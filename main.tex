\begin{abstract}
Morphology-based screening of previously unseen treatments must decide not only which response group a sample belongs to, but whether the prediction can be trusted and how much imaging is worth acquiring. We formulate this as a reliability-aware policy that accepts a prediction, aggregates additional replicate wells, or defers it for re-measurement, and evaluate it for 37 immune and vascular stimuli in public RxRx2 HUVEC Cell Painting data with entire treatments held out. A 6~KB Logistic model on released embeddings reaches balanced accuracy 0.442, but chemokine and angiogenic responses are nearly indistinguishable. Under treatment-level paired bootstrap, aggregating six replicate wells improves balanced accuracy by 0.049 (95\% CI 0.005--0.096), and nested confidence-based deferral lowers accepted-case error by 0.046 (0.005--0.089) at 71\% coverage. Experiment shift compounds treatment shift, reducing balanced accuracy to 0.374 even for treatments seen in training. Reducing raw-image input from six to three channels lowers balanced accuracy from 0.398 to 0.362 while leaving inference compute almost unchanged, so its benefit lies in staining, acquisition, and storage cost.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Cell Painting, high-content screening, treatment shift, selective prediction, batch effects, lightweight inference.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
High-content microscopy converts cellular responses to perturbations into images and learned representations. Cell Painting provides multiplexed structural measurements that support mechanism-oriented screening, but its biological signal can be confounded by experimental and imaging variation \cite{cimini2023,arevalo2024}. In the immune phenomics work associated with RxRx2, soluble factors with related function already showed related morphology in high-dimensional representation space \cite{cuccarese2020}. A practical screening pipeline must therefore decide not only which broad response group is predicted, but also whether the prediction is reliable enough to act on.

This question becomes sharper as image-based assays move from dedicated high-content systems toward compact and lower-cost imaging devices. Each additional fluorescence channel requires its own stain, excitation source, filter set, and exposure time, so channel count directly drives device cost, acquisition time, and data volume. A lightweight screening device can only replace part of a full assay if it knows when its own prediction is unreliable and when additional measurements are worth their cost.

Prior Cell Painting workflows emphasize replicate reproducibility and batch correction because technical variation can obscure perturbation effects \cite{bray2016,cimini2023,arevalo2024}. However, broad functional screening of a previously unseen soluble factor introduces a distinct operational question: when should a low-cost prediction be accepted, averaged across additional wells, or deferred for follow-up measurement? Our contribution is therefore a screening \emph{policy} rather than a new classifier: each prediction passes through explicit acquisition, aggregation, and deferral decisions, and every decision is evaluated on stimuli never seen during training. Specifically, we contribute: 1) an expert-reviewed four-group mapping of 37 immune and vascular stimuli (Table~\ref{tab:mapping}) with group-specific reporting that exposes biases hidden by aggregate scores; 2) a treatment-held-out evaluation of the policy's reliability controls, namely replicate-well aggregation, plate awareness, and nested confidence-based deferral, with treatment-level paired confidence intervals, under both treatment and experiment shift; and 3) a raw-image 6/3/2-channel ablation that separates acquisition burden from inference cost. Replicate averaging, plate centering, and selective prediction are established techniques; our aim is to quantify how far each can be trusted when combined in a lightweight policy for unseen stimuli. We do not claim the first functional map of these stimuli.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\subsection{Data and functional labels}
We use released RxRx2 HUVEC metadata and image embeddings \cite{rxrx2}, together with the raw six-channel images of all sites belonging to the analysed treatments, downloaded from the public archive. Functional labels were drafted by a pharmacy student and confirmed by a professor of pharmacy, grouping 37 treatments into type I/II interferons, IL-1/TNF-superfamily cytokines, CXC/CC chemokines, and angiogenic/vascular-regulatory factors (Table~\ref{tab:mapping}). Type-III interferon ligands IL-28A, IL-28B, and IL-29 were excluded during this review because of limited expected HUVEC responsiveness. The chemokine group contains twice as many treatments as the others. Each treatment is applied at six concentrations in two HUVEC experiments (HUVEC-1 and HUVEC-2), giving 444 treatment--concentration--experiment conditions with six replicate wells each. The embedding set contains 10,642 image sites from 2,664 wells on 24 plates.

\subsection{Evaluation protocols and models}
All experiments hold out entire treatments, so no image of a test stimulus is seen during training. \emph{Embedding experiments} use five-fold stratified group cross-validation with treatment as the group and pool out-of-fold predictions over all 37 treatments. \emph{Raw-image experiments} use one fixed split of 23 training, 5 validation, and 9 test treatments, with every group represented in each split, because repeated CNN training per fold was computationally impractical. Absolute scores from the two protocols are therefore not directly comparable. We report macro-F1 and balanced accuracy (BA), together with expected calibration error (ECE, ten bins). All 95\% confidence intervals use a treatment-level cluster bootstrap (1,000 resamples of treatments); for comparisons between two settings, both are evaluated on the same resampled treatments, giving a paired interval for the difference.

On released embeddings we benchmark standardized, class-weighted Logistic Regression; XGBoost \cite{chen2016} without class weighting (250 trees, depth 4, learning rate 0.06, row and column subsampling 0.85); and a small MLP (hidden layers of 64 and 32 units, trained on class-balanced resampled data with early stopping). For dimensional reduction, principal component analysis is fitted inside each training fold. Inference cost is measured as single-site latency on a laptop CPU (Intel Core Ultra 7 255H) together with the serialized model size.

For raw images, we fine-tune ImageNet-pretrained MobileNetV3-Small \cite{howard2019}. Its first convolution is replaced by a $C$-channel convolution initialized with the channel-averaged RGB filter scaled by $3/C$, and the classifier is replaced by a four-way output. Each site is resized to $224\times224$, augmented with random flips and $90^\circ$ rotations, and sampled with class-balanced weights. Training uses AdamW (learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), batch size 32, and up to 25 epochs with early stopping (patience 5) on validation performance. We compare all six channels against a three-channel DNA/RNA/mitochondria configuration and a two-channel DNA/mitochondria configuration; the channel subsets were fixed before inspecting test results. Six- and three-channel models are repeated with two random seeds; the two-channel configuration is exploratory with one seed.

\subsection{Reliability analyses}
\emph{Replicate aggregation} (Logistic). Four sites are averaged into a well embedding, and $k$ wells of the same condition are averaged into a condition embedding. For $k\in\{1,2,3\}$ the wells are drawn at random ten times and results are averaged; $k=6$ uses all wells and is deterministic. The same 444 conditions are evaluated for every $k$. Concentration strata are defined within each treatment by ranking its six concentrations: the two lowest form the \emph{low} stratum and the two highest the \emph{high} stratum.

\emph{Plate awareness} (Logistic). We compare cosine similarity of same-condition well pairs on the same versus different plates, and evaluate unlabeled plate centering, which subtracts each plate's mean embedding and requires no labels.

\emph{Confidence-based deferral} (XGBoost, chosen for its calibration). Following selective prediction \cite{geifman2017}, a site is accepted when its maximum class probability exceeds a threshold $\tau$. In nested deferral, one complete training treatment per group is withheld in each outer fold as a calibration set, and the lowest $\tau$ whose calibration-set error does not exceed 0.40 is applied unchanged to the unseen outer test fold. We additionally report the risk--coverage curve and its area (AURC) from pooled out-of-fold predictions.

\emph{Group and shift diagnostics.} We evaluate a three-class variant that merges chemokine and angiogenic groups, and a binary chemokine-versus-angiogenic classifier. For experiment shift, models are trained on HUVEC-1 and tested on HUVEC-2, both within the treatment-held-out folds and in a diagnostic that allows the same treatments in both experiments.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Embedding baselines and group-specific behavior}
Table~\ref{tab:embedding} summarizes treatment-held-out performance on released embeddings. The small MLP is poorly calibrated (ECE 0.477) and unsuitable for confidence-based deferral. Treatment-level intervals are wide and overlap across all three models, so the ranking should not be over-interpreted. Reducing the Logistic input to 32 principal components retains BA (0.446), whereas 16 components lower it to 0.431.

Similar BA hides very different group behavior (Table~\ref{tab:recall}). The class-weighted Logistic model recognizes interferon and IL-1/TNF responses with recall near 0.51, but confuses chemokine and angiogenic responses with each other almost symmetrically. The unweighted XGBoost model instead assigns most sites of every group to the chemokine majority, reaching chemokine recall 0.92 but angiogenic recall 0.06. The four-class score therefore mainly reflects separation of interferon and IL-1/TNF responses; in this cell type, chemokine and angiogenic stimuli do not produce distinguishable morphology in the released embeddings.

\subsection{Replicate aggregation is most valuable under low concentration}
Averaging all six wells instead of one raises condition-level BA from 0.416 to 0.465 (Fig.~\ref{fig:replicates}); the paired difference is $+0.049$ (95\% CI 0.005--0.096). The gain doubles in the low-concentration stratum, from 0.294 to 0.396 ($+0.103$, CI 0.017--0.190), but is small in the high stratum (0.499 to 0.509). Replicate acquisition is therefore most useful exactly where morphology signals are weak, which matches the single-site pattern in which XGBoost BA rises from 0.282 (low) to 0.550 (high).

\subsection{Plate awareness and confidence-based deferral}
Same-condition well pairs are more similar on the same plate (cosine 0.885) than across plates (0.862). Unlabeled plate centering raises Logistic BA from 0.443 to 0.450 (this independent re-run differs from Table~\ref{tab:embedding} by 0.001); the paired gain is small but consistent ($+0.007$, CI 0.002--0.012).

For nested deferral, pooling the five outer test folds gives accepted-case error 0.404 at 70.9\% coverage versus 0.451 without deferral, a paired reduction of 0.046 (CI 0.005--0.089). The selection rule was nevertheless unstable: because each calibration set contains only four treatments, thresholds ranged from 0.45 to 0.60 and coverage from 37\% to 81\% across folds, and the accepted error exceeded the 0.40 calibration-set target in two of five folds (0.438 and 0.478). Fig.~\ref{fig:riskcoverage} shows that the pooled risk--coverage curve decreases steadily, with an AURC of 0.302 for XGBoost compared with its overall error of 0.452. Below roughly 15\% coverage the error approaches zero, but about half of these confident sites come from four strong-response cytokines (IL-1$\alpha$, IL-1$\beta$, TNF-$\alpha$, IFN-$\omega$), so this regime reflects easy treatments rather than a generally safe operating point. Agreement among sites of the same well did not provide a monotonic reliability signal (error by consistency quartile 0.467, 0.440, 0.484, 0.418). We therefore interpret deferral as an exploratory re-measurement triage policy rather than a fixed decision rule.

\subsection{Experiment shift compounds treatment shift}
Training on HUVEC-1 and testing on HUVEC-2 within the treatment-held-out folds lowers BA to 0.365 (Logistic), 0.344 (XGBoost), and 0.325 (small MLP), compared with 0.417--0.442 when both experiments are pooled. When the same treatments are allowed in both experiments, BA still reaches only 0.374 (Logistic) and 0.368 (XGBoost). Thus, even stimuli seen during training are recognized less reliably in a new experiment than unseen stimuli within pooled experiments. Practical screening faces treatment novelty and experiment-level variation simultaneously, which strengthens the case for replicate acquisition and deferral over a single model score.

\subsection{Reduced-channel raw-image screening}
Table~\ref{tab:channels} and Fig.~\ref{fig:channels} summarize raw-image ablations on the fixed treatment-held-out split. The six-channel configuration was strongest and stable across seeds (BA $0.398\pm0.002$). Reducing input to DNA/RNA/mitochondria lowered BA to $0.362\pm0.003$; the single-seed two-channel result (0.365) is exploratory and does not rank two versus three channels. Because only the first convolution depends on the input channel count, the network barely changes: it has 1.52~M parameters in every configuration, compute falls only from 60.9 to 55.5 and 53.7~MMACs, and single-thread CPU latency from 7.9 to 7.6 and 7.5~ms per site. The saving of reduced-channel imaging therefore lies in stains, optics, acquisition time, and image data volume (one half or one third of the six-channel data), not in inference, and it comes at a measurable accuracy cost.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The four groups are biologically reviewed but not equally separable in HUVEC morphology: chemokine and angiogenic stimuli are confused regardless of the classifier, so a screening policy should report group-specific reliability, and a three-group formulation may be more appropriate for this cell type. The effect sizes of the reliability controls are modest and their intervals are wide because only 37 treatments are available; the paired intervals exclude zero, but they should be confirmed on larger treatment panels. The deferral rule did not consistently meet its calibration-set target, indicating that larger calibration sets are needed before a threshold can be fixed in advance. Only two HUVEC experiments are available, so experiment shift on independently acquired systems remains to be measured. The raw-image comparison uses a single split with nine test treatments; seed variation is small, but variation across alternative splits is not captured. Finally, all images come from a high-content research platform; the channel ablation emulates, but does not demonstrate, a lower-cost imaging device. The system should therefore be framed as reliability-aware candidate screening and re-measurement prioritization, not clinical diagnosis or definitive mechanism annotation.

\section{Conclusion}
For morphology-based screening of unseen stimuli, the central question is not only how accurate a classifier is but when its prediction can be trusted. Under combined treatment and experiment shift, replicate-well aggregation and confidence-based deferral yield modest but statistically supported reliability gains, plate centering adds a small consistent improvement, and group-specific reporting reveals that chemokine and angiogenic responses are not separable in HUVEC morphology. Six-channel imaging offered the strongest raw-image result; reducing to three or two channels lowers stain, optics, acquisition, and storage burden at a measurable accuracy cost while leaving inference cost essentially unchanged. Future work should validate this policy prospectively, with larger treatment panels, and on independently acquired lower-cost imaging systems.
